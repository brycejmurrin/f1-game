# Explore the eras

Session designs for Apex 26 Learn · Evidence checked 23 November 2026

Start with **What replaced DRS?** It gives players a question about a familiar control and a quick comparison they can repeat. **Why did the floor change?** and **What limits engine performance?** build on the distinction between what is physically possible and what a rule allows. **How do safety layers work?** uses an incident investigation. **Who gets the next upgrade?** connects the eras to development restrictions. Each session opens with a worked example and takes roughly two to four minutes without its optional transfer question. No session gates the next. The order is a proposed learning sequence. [Learn plan](plan.md)

Alternate a short worked example with one independent choice. Put the diagram beside a literal readout of the same state, let the learner explain or predict the next result, and offer a new case after feedback. This applies the What Works Clearinghouse guidance on worked examples, integrated graphics and words, retrieval, and explanatory questions. Skip keeps the controls available. Completion means the learner has reached the result, while the optional check records which distinction they understood. [IES practice guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/1/Published)

## 1. What replaced DRS?

**Learning objective.** Identify the part that moves, the conditions that enable it, and the separate source of passing assistance in each season.

1. Open at a synthetic 0.8 s time gap with a dry race and the relevant controls enabled. An overview places the 2025 DRS detection and activation pairs on a simplified Las Vegas outline. Switch to 2026 to reveal a distinct Straight Mode zone layer and electrical Overtake markers at 5,299 m and 5,400 m. The maps and markers are sourced. Car trajectories and gaps in this lesson are scripted.
2. Work the 2025 example. At a DRS detection point, show the measured gap and save the eligibility result for that zone. Use **Advance to activation** to expose a rear-wing-only DRS action. Show the follower's flap opening and leave the leader's flap closed in this two-car fixture. Add a status explanation when DRS is disabled, and keep race eligibility distinct from practice and qualifying.
3. In the 2026 version of the fixture, switch between 0.8 s and 1.2 s at the Overtake detection marker. At activation, show Overtake eligible at 0.8 s and unavailable at 1.2 s in the verified 1.0 s event configuration. In a separate panel, show both drivers permitted to use Straight Mode inside an enabled dry zone, regardless of the selected following gap. Draw front and rear wing states. Moving the gap **after** detection leaves the sampled outcome intact for the pending activation.
4. Pause an illustrative 2026 run at 330 km/h outside alternative power sectors, dry and with sufficient energy. Compare the ordinary electrical deployment ceiling of 150 kW with the Overtake ceiling of 350 kW. Compute the ordinary ceiling using the Section C curve, 1,800 − 5 × speed in km/h in this interval, and keep speed constant when comparing the two bars. At 350 km/h the ordinary ceiling is zero while eligible Overtake is capped at 100 kW. Allow a depleted-store preset to show zero modeled delivery despite unchanged eligibility. A bar describes available electrical power under the rule and fixture, with passing outcome intentionally outside the model.
5. **Transfer check.** “You measured 1.2 s at detection and closed to 0.8 s before activation. Which 2026 action is available in its enabled dry zone?” Award the check when the learner selects Straight Mode and cites the sampled gap as the reason Overtake stayed unavailable. Optional advanced variant switches to Low Grip and shows Overtake disabled, with the event’s eligible partial wing configuration and zones.

**Outputs and boundary tests.** Label each marker with its system and effect. At 2025 DRS 0.999 s show eligible and at exactly 1.000 s show ineligible under the less-than-one-second rule. For 2026 ship 0.8 s and 1.2 s as primary teaching presets. If adding an exact-threshold fixture, derive its comparator directly from B7.2. Confirm both cars can use Straight Mode in the 1.2 s dry fixture and that missing data leaves an explicit unavailable reason. The map layer can use the same schematic layout with separate season-scoped marker sets. Use **Available**, **Active** and **Unavailable**, with legal state, driver action and outcome represented distinctly. ## 2. Why did the floor change?

**Learning objective.** Explain how the underbody geometry changes the available means of making downforce, and distinguish geometry requirements from an engineering performance result.

1. Start with a side-by-side cutaway of a 1978 Lotus 79-type underbody and a 1983 flat-bottom design. Ask the player to tap **Where does air travel under the car?** On the 1978 diagram, shade the constriction and expansion and let the player toggle the schematic side seal between intact and leaking. The live readout moves from a stronger to a weaker pressure difference with the leak. Use qualitative arrows with a shared scale and visible simplified-diagram label.
2. Let the player fit the same shaped floor to the 1983 plate. Highlight the flat central floor zone and explain the period rule's geometry. The assembly state becomes **incompatible with this rule**. A second animation shows a legal flat central floor with rear expansion and external wings so learners can locate other sources of downforce.
3. Switch to 2022 and 2026. Keep vehicle outline scale consistent and show the 2022 shaped floor tunnels and the FIA’s shallower, less dependent 2026 concept. Offer **Open wings** only in the modern panel, with airflow and corner/straight readouts in a separate row. Define downforce as vertical load and drag as force opposing motion. A tap spot explains why two cars with similar body outlines can have very different underfloor flow.
4. Open the 2009 double-diffuser appeal as an optional evidence question. Show a diagram that lets the learner highlight each opening and each prescribed plane. Ask whether declared intent alone resolves a dispute. Reveal the FIA International Court of Appeal’s outcome and its rule interpretation. Keep anecdotal claims about exact lap-time gains out of this case.
5. **Transfer check.** Given a synthetic design with larger tunnels and intact sealing, pick two things still needed to estimate its effect on lap performance. Accept speed and ride-condition/aero response as the worked pair, and explain what the geometry panel establishes about applicability. If a learner chooses a launch-project percentage, open its source as design intent.

**Outputs and scope.** Ship four period plates plus a comparison mode that changes one of floor geometry, sealing or wings at a time. Keep legal status, qualitative airflow and any measured data in separate labelled fields. Omit a ride-height slider until there is a defensible fixture for separation, stall or porpoising. Avoid implying an exact historical pressure field or a universal cross-era downforce percentage. ## 3. What limits engine performance?

**Learning objective.** Distinguish displacement, boost ceiling, fuel quantity, fuel energy flow, electrical power and electrical energy.

1. Offer a worked **1988 Honda RA168E** pit-wall note. Assign a 2.5 bar maximum boost and 150 L race fuel ceiling to the turbo car. Let the learner set hypothetical boost and starting fuel independently. Mark 2.6 bar or 151 L out of bounds. Flip to the 3.5 L naturally aspirated option permitted in 1988, then into the 1989 formula with turbo eligibility removed. Honda’s case establishes the regulatory inputs. The exercise never makes a claim about fuel consumption or predicted lap time.
2. Switch to a simplified 2014–25 hybrid schematic. Route arrows between the turbo and MGU-H and between the wheels and MGU-K. Switch to 2026 and let the learner trace one fewer recovery path. Show the historic 120 kW MGU-K maximum beside the 2026 absolute 350 kW DC limit. Label the 3,000 MJ/h fuel-energy-flow ceiling as fuel energy input. An optional conversion reveals roughly 833 kW fuel energy input at that ceiling before losses.
3. Start a synthetic run at 2.0 MJ usable charge, ignoring recharge and losses in the first exercise. A request for 350 kW over four seconds consumes 1.4 MJ and leaves 0.6 MJ. A later three-second request at 350 kW uses the remaining 0.6 MJ in about 1.71 seconds and then clips. Move the second request into a different window and show both on the same time axis. Pin a low enough speed and full-deployment fixture so the 350 kW ceiling applies before store depletion.
4. Add charging only in an optional case. Use 2026 Las Vegas event limits of 6.0 MJ in qualifying and 8.0/8.5 MJ in race with Overtake inactive/active. Teach that lap-total recharge and the 4 MJ usable state-of-charge swing can differ because energy can enter and leave repeatedly. The simple model is a bookkeeping demonstration and leaves torque, conversion losses and qualifying optimization outside its predictions.
5. **Transfer check.** Ask whether 350 kW can describe energy consumed. Have the learner select duration and compute 0.7 MJ over two seconds, then explain why legal instantaneous power and energy available over the lap answer different questions.

**Outputs and constraints.** Four separate fields hold race fuel, fuel energy-flow rate, electrical power and electrical energy. Internal values use explicit units and the display rounds only after calculations. Chart delivered and requested power separately. Verify the zero-energy boundary, one full 2 s window, and store depletion partway through a window. When pairing 2014 and 2026 diagrams, pin each claim to its season and explain which arrows represent the electric machine versus energy flow. ## 4. How do safety layers work?

**Learning objective.** Match a protection layer to its role and distinguish a documented finding from a counterfactual.

1. Open a quiet schematic of the survival cell, Halo, HANS, harness, barrier and response team. Show an early timeline card for the 1981 carbon-fibre monocoque and a card for 2018 Halo adoption. Each interactive hotspot can be reached through a parallel text list.
2. Play the worked 2020 Bahrain sequence in three optional static frames: contact and barrier impact, cockpit protection, and driver exit and medical response. Select **Protect cockpit space** to highlight the survival cell and Halo. Select **Manage forces on the driver** to show HANS and harness. Select **Limit fire exposure and respond** to show fire clothing and response. The debrief paraphrases findings attributed to the FIA investigation.
3. Ask which evidence supports a role. Provide cards for survival space, fuel-system damage, and response. A wrong choice keeps the sourced finding visible and asks the learner to connect it to the right stage. Do not reconstruct injury probabilities or offer a “remove the Halo” outcome.
4. **Transfer check.** For the 2021 Monza collision, show the FIA steward decision beside a schematic. Ask whether that decision is evidence about the sporting responsibility for contact, measured loads through the Halo, or both. Accept sporting responsibility. Invite the learner to choose an appropriate source for a protective-load claim, such as an incident investigation with documented measurements.

**Outputs and constraints.** The interaction may be completed as text. No graphic injury imagery or replayed crash sound. Each highlight has a verb and target and the source role is explicit. Keep the rule-change timeline independent of the documented contribution of any component in a specific incident. ## 5. Who gets the next upgrade?

**Learning objective.** Recognize budget and aerodynamic testing as separate constraints and explain the difference between a championship allowance and a sanction.

1. Use two fictional teams in 2026. Select P1 and P7. Start with the Section F reference of 320 wind-tunnel runs per period, giving 224 at P1 and 320 at P7. An instructional upgrade asks for 30 runs. Give them 20 and 40 runs of remaining allowance respectively. The stations show **10 runs over** and **10 runs left** when the project is selected.
2. Open the independent spending card. The teaching budget has 2.0 million dollars of Relevant Costs headroom for the first team and 0.8 million for the second, and the project uses 1.0 million. Update to **1.0 million left** and **0.2 million over**. Offer a reduced testing plan and a lower-cost alternate design as two authored trade-offs. A project can proceed only when both constraints pass.
3. Compare with the 2021 introduction. Open the actual Red Bull Accepted Breach Agreement and select the sanction lever. It reduced the applicable coefficient by 10% for 12 months and imposed a US$7 million fine. A labelled demonstration multiplies the 2026 illustrative P1 coefficient 70% by 0.9 to reach 63%. Keep the real agreement on a separate dated card to prevent a claim that the illustrative modern coefficient was Red Bull’s historical allocation.
4. **Transfer check.** Ask whether moving from P1 to P7 increases the team’s cost cap in this fixture. Accept “wind-tunnel allowance increases and the cost cap is calculated separately.” Advanced prompt asks why the public reporting numbers for the 2021 Red Bull case can include both £1,864,000 and £432,652. Reveal the FIA's accepted findings and the notional-tax-credit calculation.

**Outputs and constraints.** Year, period and finishing position stay visible. Keep dollar and run gates separate. Accept an exactly-at-limit project when both nonnegative balances reach zero. Only calculate season cap from a chosen edition, event count and relevant assumptions. The fictional costs are already classified for this exercise. A full compliance tool would need the reporting and exclusion rules.
